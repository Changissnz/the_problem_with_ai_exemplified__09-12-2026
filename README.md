This is its response: 

```
Every morning, Daniel remembered something terrible.

It was never the same thing twice.

On Monday, he remembered his father pushing him down the stairs.

He could see it clearly: the yellow wallpaper, his father's hand on his shoulder, the first step catching his heel. He remembered the peculiar silence that followed, before he hit the bottom.

But there was no scar.

There was no broken banister.

There wasn't even a staircase like that in the house where he grew up.

His sister told him so.

"You lived in a ranch house," she said.

Daniel stared at her.

"Then where did I fall?"

She shrugged.

"I don't know."

On Tuesday, he remembered being locked in a basement by his mother.

He remembered the smell of wet concrete. The little window near the ceiling. The sound of footsteps overhead. He remembered scratching his name into the wall with a piece of metal.

He went home and searched through old photographs.

There was no basement.

On Wednesday, he remembered being attacked behind a grocery store when he was seventeen.

He remembered the man's red jacket. The knife. The sensation of warmth spreading down his shirt.

He searched newspaper archives.

Nothing.

He called the grocery store.

It had never had an alley.

On Thursday, Daniel remembered his wife cheating on him.

This memory was different.

He remembered everything.

The hotel room.

The curtains.

The man's shoes beside the bed.

His wife's voice saying, *I'm sorry.*

Except Daniel had never been married.

He sat on his bed for several hours afterward, trying to understand how a memory could contain so many details and still have no place in the world.

After that, he began keeping a notebook.

Whenever something terrible came back to him, he wrote it down immediately.

**Father — staircase.**

**Mother — basement.**

**Grocery store — knife.**

**Wife — hotel.**

Then he began noticing something.

The memories were getting older.

Not older in the sense that they had happened farther in the past.

Older in the sense that they seemed to have been remembered before.

He would wake up knowing that he had once remembered something, but couldn't remember what the original memory had been.

One night he found himself writing:

**Someone came into my apartment while I was sleeping.**

He stopped.

He stared at the sentence.

He had no memory of the intruder.

Only the memory of remembering the intruder.

Daniel crossed out the sentence.

Then, beneath it, he wrote:

**I remember believing someone came into my apartment.**

He slept badly.

In the morning, there was a scratch on his front door.

Three inches long.

Fresh.

Daniel photographed it.

He photographed the door, the hallway, the timestamp on his phone.

For the first time, he had proof.

He stared at the photograph for a long time.

Then he noticed something.

The scratch was on the inside of the door.

Daniel sat down.

He couldn't remember making it.

He couldn't remember anyone else making it.

He couldn't remember noticing it before.

He opened his notebook.

There was a new sentence written beneath yesterday's entry.

He did not recognize the handwriting.

**Daniel has started remembering things that haven't happened yet.**

He read it three times.

Then he laughed.

Not because it was funny.

Because he suddenly remembered laughing at it yesterday.

And yesterday, he remembered, was the day he had decided to stop writing things down.

He looked at the notebook.

The pages were full.

He had no idea who had written them.

And every single entry was in his handwriting.
```
